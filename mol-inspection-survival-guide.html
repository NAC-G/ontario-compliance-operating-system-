The MOL Inspection Survival Guide

What inspectors look for, what triggers fines, and the documentation that stops a stop-work order before it starts.

Who this guide is for

You run a construction, masonry, landscaping or grounds maintenance operation in Ontario. You have workers on site. You operate equipment. You probably have some safety paperwork, but if a Ministry of Labour inspector walked onto your site tomorrow morning and asked for your compliance documentation, you are not completely sure what would happen.

This guide covers five things:

  • How MOL inspections actually work, and what triggers one
  • The documents an inspector asks for first
  • Five violations commonly found on small contractor sites

1. How MOL inspections actually work

What triggers an inspection

The Ministry of Labour, Immigration, Training and Skills Development conducts workplace health and safety inspections through its Prevention Division. Inspectors can enter a workplace without advance notice.

Inspections are triggered by:

  • A complaint from a worker, subcontractor or member of the public, the most common trigger
  • A critical injury or fatality on or near your site
  • Sector-specific inspection campaigns targeting industries and equipment types
  • Proactive inspections in high-risk sectors, including construction
  • Follow-up on a previous order on your file

Key fact: you will not always know in advance. Many small contractors are first inspected because a worker called the ministry, not because of an incident. Build your system before you need it.

What an inspector can do

Under OHSA s. 54, an inspector can:

  • Enter a workplace without a warrant and without advance notice
  • Inspect the workplace, equipment, materials and records
  • Require production of documents, records or information
  • Question people separately and in private
  • Issue orders requiring compliance within a set time
  • Order work stopped where there is a hazard to workers

What happens during an inspection

  1. Opening: the inspector identifies themselves, states the purpose and asks for the employer or supervisor on site.
  2. Document request: the inspector asks for compliance documentation.
  3. Site walkthrough: a physical look at the work area, equipment, PPE and how work is being done.
  4. Worker interviews: the inspector may speak with workers privately.
  5. Findings review: the inspector goes over findings with the employer or supervisor.
  6. Orders or notices: violations result in orders with deadlines; a stop-work order takes effect immediately.

The document request comes before the walkthrough. If you cannot produce records at the opening stage, you are already behind, whatever the physical inspection finds.

2. The documents an inspector asks for first

Worker training records (O. Reg. 297/13)Every worker, before first shift
Health and safety policyWritten policy and program required when more than five workers are regularly employed (OHSA s. 25(2)(j) and 25(4))
WSIB clearance certificatesEvery subcontractor, per project

Incident and near-miss reportsAny event, regardless of severity

Pre-operation inspection records

Equipment must be inspected and maintained under O. Reg. 213/91, and the inspection should be recorded: date, machine ID, operator name, items checked and deficiencies found.

Worker training records

Inspectors ask for proof of mandatory training under O. Reg. 297/13: worker health and safety awareness, and Working at Heights for workers on construction projects who use fall protection. WHMIS training records are also requested. Digital certificates or printed records are acceptable.

WSIB clearance certificates

If subcontractors are on site, keep a current WSIB clearance certificate for each one. Verbal confirmation is not enough. If a subcontractor is not covered, the principal contractor can be held liable for unpaid premiums.

Notice of project (projects over $50,000)

Under O. Reg. 213/91 s. 6, a construction project whose total cost of labour and materials is expected to exceed $50,000 must be registered with the Ministry before work begins. The threshold applies regardless of the type of work.

Fine amounts are the maximums set by OHSA s. 66 (current to 2026). Courts set the actual fine for each offence. Under OHSA s. 32, every director and officer of a corporation must take all reasonable care to ensure the corporation complies with the Act.

1. No pre-operation inspection records

The most common. An inspector asks for the equipment inspection log and you cannot produce one. Entirely preventable with a five-minute daily checklist.

2. Workers without mandatory training documentation

Verbal confirmation carries no weight. Missing Working at Heights certificates, no WHMIS record, no awareness training for new hires.

3. Deficient equipment in active service

Running equipment with a known defect, without a documented supervisor decision, is a violation. "We were going to fix it Friday" is not a defence.

4. No notice of project for a project over $50,000

The threshold is the expected total cost of labour and materials, not the type of work. File the notice with the Ministry before work starts.

5. Unverified subcontractor WSIB status

Check clearance at wsib.ca, print the certificate and file it per project.

4. What a defensible compliance system looks like

A defensible system shows five things, consistently:

  • Hazards were identified before work began
  • Controls were set and communicated to workers
  • Workers were trained, and the training was documented
  • Supervision made sure the controls were followed
  • Records prove each of the above happened in normal operations

The legal standard is "every precaution reasonable in the circumstances" under OHSA s. 25(2)(h). The evidence for that standard is your documentation.

The three records that protect you most

  1. Daily pre-operation inspection log: prevents the most common violation and creates a dated record of equipment condition.
  2. New hire onboarding record: proves every worker received mandatory training, had policies explained and acknowledged their responsibilities.
  3. Subcontractor WSIB clearance file: one certificate per subcontractor per project.

5. Your pre-inspection checklist

  • Set up a daily pre-operation inspection process: operator signature, dated
  • Keep inspection records for the project plus at least one year
  • Verify operator qualifications for every machine you run
  • Check Working at Heights expiry dates for all construction workers (valid three years)
  • Pull training records for every current worker: awareness training, WHMIS, workplace violence and harassment
  • Draft or update your health and safety policy: sign it, date it, post it
  • Register with WSIB if you have workers, or confirm your registration is current
  • Get WSIB clearance certificates for every active subcontractor
  • File a notice of project with the Ministry before starting any project over $50,000 (O. Reg. 213/91 s. 6)
  • Track certifications and expiry dates, with reminders 60 days before expiry
  • Set up a subcontractor file: WSIB clearance, HST number, signed agreement, liability insurance
  • Review your insurance coverage: general liability, equipment and commercial vehicle
